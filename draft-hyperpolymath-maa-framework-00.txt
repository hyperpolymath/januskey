                                                            Abstract

The widespread reliance on unverifiable, best-effort transactional
integrity and data deletion mechanisms in decentralized systems
constitutes a systemic trust deficit. This deficit leads to chronic
compliance failures (e.g., GDPR Article 17) and unrecoverable state
corruption in Edge and IoT deployments. This document introduces the
Mutually Assured Accountability (MAA) Framework, an ethical and
engineering paradigm. MAA operationalises the **Maximal Principle
Reduction (MPR)** axiom by proposing a pair of dual primitives (RMR
and RMO) intended to balance auditability and user autonomy. Each
property claimed for them below is stated with its current evidence
status, and none of them is proved in full: reversibility is proved
only for a model and tested for single steps, and unrecoverability
after erasure is not proved.

                                   1. Introduction

The principles governing data management in high-assurance,
decentralized systems remain fundamentally reliant on operational
accountability methods (logging, monitoring, eventual consistency).
These methods do not provide **epistemological certainty**, the
mathematical guarantee that a system state is correct, or that a
deleted record no longer exists. This gap leads to structural trust
deficits.

The MAA Framework addresses this by proposing two symmetrical
primitives derived from the MPR axiom. They are implemented in Rust
in this repository (crates/reversible-core, crates/januskey-cli).
No other language or toolchain enforces them: nothing here is
compiled by, constrained by, or depends on the Oblíbený toolchain
that the -00 text named.

* **RMR (Reversible Transaction):** A primitive for state transitions
  that records, before each change, what is needed to undo it. Its
  reversibility claim is relative to a stated equality on states and
  has two directions (Section 3). It supports auditability and
  dependability.

* **RMO (Obliterative Wipe):** A primitive for data erasure that
  overwrites and removes the data and leaves a tamper-evident record
  that it did so. It does not prove that the data no longer exists
  (Section 4).

The MAA dynamic dictates that the deployment of RMR (Accountability)
must be coupled with RMO (Control).

                                   2. Terminology

The key terms defined within this document are:

* **MAA (Mutually Assured Accountability):** The ethical and
  engineering paradigm that balances adversarial system requirements
  by pairing a reversible primitive with an erasing one.

* **MPR (Maximal Principle Reduction):** The core axiom used to
  constrain system complexity to a minimal, checkable subset. It is a
  design philosophy; no tool in this repository enforces it.

* **RMR (Reversible Transaction):** A data primitive that records, for
  each state change F, the information that determines an inverse
  F^-1. Section 3 states exactly what is claimed about F^-1.

* **RMO (Obliterative Wipe):** A data primitive that overwrites and
  removes specified data and records the fact. Whether the data is
  unrecoverable afterwards is not established (Section 4).

                                3. MAA and Formal Integrity

Current distributed transaction models (e.g., Two-Phase Commit, Saga)
are complex and can enter blocking or inconsistent states. RMR
proposes a simpler mechanism: each operation records the information
that determines its inverse before it changes anything.

3.1. The equality the claim is relative to

Every reversibility statement in this section is an equality up to an
equivalence ~ on states, and it says nothing about any finer equality.
Two equivalences are in use:

  ~tree   Two states are ~tree-equal when the regular files under the
          working tree have the same relative paths and the same byte
          content. Permission bits, owner and group, timestamps,
          symlink identity, inode identity and empty directories are
          not compared. This is what the property test
          execute_then_undo_is_identity checks. The implementation
          also restores permission bits on undo, but no test compares
          them, so they are not part of ~tree. Owner, group, mtime
          and symlink identity are recorded but not restored.

  ~model  Propositional equality on the abstract state type of
          verification/proofs/agda/TransactionAtomicity.agda. Nothing
          links that type to the filesystem mechanically.

3.2. The claim, in both directions

Let F be an operation, D_F the states on which its precondition holds,
and F^-1 the inverse determined by the metadata F recorded.

  RMR-L (left inverse):  for every s in D_F,       F^-1(F(s)) ~ s
  RMR-R (right inverse): for every t in F(D_F),    F(F^-1(t)) ~ t

Together they say that F, restricted to D_F, is a bijection onto its
image up to ~. RMR-R ranges over the image F(D_F) only, not over every
state on which F^-1 is defined: Copy^-1 is Delete of the destination,
and copying again does not restore arbitrary destination content.
RMR-R follows from RMR-L when F respects ~ (s ~ s' implies
F(s) ~ F(s')), and that congruence is not stated or tested either.

3.3. What is established today

  RMR-L, one step, ~tree    TESTED for Delete, Modify, Move, Copy and
                            Create (property test above). Chmod is
                            not in that test; Chown undo is not
                            implemented. A failed execute leaving the
                            state unchanged is ASSUMED (ledger T-6).
  RMR-L, sequences, ~model  PROVEN as ledger T-1
                            (TransactionAtomicity.rollback-restores),
                            given that each step's undo restores its
                            execute (T-6, ASSUMED).
  RMR-R                     No ledger row. Not proved, not tested:
                            OPEN.
  "Every operation is       OPEN.
   reversible"
  Obliteration              Excluded by construction: a transaction
                            containing one is not all-reversible
                            (ledger C-3, PROVEN in the model).

The guarantee is therefore not that the primitive "is structurally
incapable of leaving the state corrupted". It is the conditional
statements above, at the strength the ledger gives them.

                                   4. MAA and User Autonomy

RMO addresses the chronic failure of data persistence to honour user
deletion requests. The primitive overwrites the data in place, removes
it, scrubs the unshared content-store blobs and log entries for it, and
records a timestamped commitment to what was destroyed. That record is
tamper-evident; it is not a certificate that the data is gone.

That an attacker cannot recover obliterated content is OPEN (ledger
E-1); only a model-level shadow of it is proved (E-1m). Hash
one-wayness and physical erasure on the storage media are ASSUMED,
external to this repository (ledger X-1). Overwrite in place does not
guarantee physical erasure on SSD, copy-on-write or journaling
filesystems. RMO is a mechanism relevant to the right to erasure; it
is not a compliance determination.

                                   5. Security Considerations

The MAA framework shifts the security focus from patch management
to structural design, by keeping the Trusted Computing Base (TCB) at
the protocol/primitive layer as small as possible. The framework is
only as strong as its proofs, which must be auditable and maintained
openly (e.g., as FOSS). The proofs in this repository are Agda
(--safe --without-K, verification/proofs/agda) and an Idris2 ABI
(src/abi). They cover the models named in Sections 3 and 4, not the
Rust implementation. This repository contains no Isabelle/HOL theory
and no Z3 script, and none of its claims rests on either.

                                   7. Acknowledgements

The principles of the MAA framework are grounded in the work on
Algorithmic Reversibility (Bennett) and the thermodynamic limits of
information erasure (Landauer). The axiomatic foundation was further
sharpened by the philosophical challenge of resolving
epistemological dilemmas (Wittgenstein and Russell) and by the
critical necessity to clearly articulate verifiable security concepts
in response to challenges raised by Joshua Jewell.
